## 1. What shipped

### Birds Phase 2 — the audio half
`17e5838` `902a17a` `df3d9e4`

The whole xeno-canto → ffmpeg → audition → upload pipeline, plus the Listen and
Match stages. 15 clips across all 10 original birds are live.

The pipeline is four npm scripts and the middle one matters:
`birds:audio-harvest` → `birds:audio-clips` (proposes a 6-second window by
band-passed loudest-window analysis, cuts a preview and a spectrogram) →
`birds:audition` (a local server on :4517 where a **human ear** confirms or
nudges every window) → `birds:audio-upload`. Nothing uploads without a
`selections.json` written by that audition. That constraint is not squeamishness:
nothing in this codebase can hear, the loudest six seconds of a field recording
can be a lawnmower, and the audition step is where a person decides which
cardinal sounds most like a cardinal.

Two judgement calls worth keeping. Clip exercises pin to the **first voice of a
kind**, because a clip reference is `{birdCode, kind}` and the Blue Jay's two
calls have opposite pitch shapes — an exercise built from the second would
contradict the clip that actually plays. And there is deliberately **no
"how many repeats" exercise**, even though the catalog carries `repeats`: that
field is the typical phrase count, any individual recording may differ, and an
exercise must never mark a child wrong for hearing the clip correctly.

### The Winter Visitors
`dd182bd`

Crew 3: Dark-eyed Junco, White-throated Sparrow, American Tree Sparrow, Purple
Finch, Cedar Waxwing. Five birds that **are not here in summer**. In August the
units do not exist; around mid-October they appear; in April they go.

Units carry `availableMonths`, **not** the flora `Season` enum, and the
difference is the entire feature: juncos reach Louisville in mid-October, which
meteorological season-splitting calls autumn. Gating on `currentSeason() ===
'winter'` would hide the crew until December, six weeks after she has been
watching them under the feeder. Out of season a unit is **absent, not
padlocked** — a padlock promises "practise and this opens", and no amount of
practice brings a junco to Kentucky in July.

Two of the five are the confusion pairs the design named, and both separate on
the calendar before any field mark: Purple Finch against the House Finch she
already knows, and American Tree Sparrow against the summer Chipping Sparrow.
The Cedar Waxwing earns its place differently — it eats berries and will never
come to a seed feeder, which is how a child learns a feeder is not the whole
world.

## 2. I deleted 1,623 of Cecily's correct answers

**The event.** `npm run db:seed`, run twice on the live database — once to
register the `bird_feeder` habitat, once after the division strand split —
deleted every `attempt` row pointing at a seed item. 1,623 correct answers for
Cecily, 338 for Esme. Garden beds unlock from lifetime-correct, so her seven open
beds collapsed to one and her 30 earned seeds to four. She opened the app to find
her gardens shut.

**Why it was possible.** `seed-math.ts` and `seed-reading.ts` deleted attempts
before re-inserting items. The script *documented* this, in a comment, and
offered `SEED_ADDITIVE=1` to avoid it. Destructive by default, safe by opt-in.

Safety that has to be remembered is not safety. I had read that same file earlier
the same day — for the strand block — and did not read as far as the item block.

**The fix** (`b4129a8`). Additive is now the default; replacing items requires
`SEED_WIPE_ITEMS=1` and says so out loud; and even that path sets
`attempt.item_id = null` rather than deleting, so a child's lifetime record can
never be destroyed by seeding again. `tests/world/seedSafety.test.ts` reads both
scripts and fails on a delete against `attempt`, on safety hidden behind an
opt-in, and on a silent replace. Same shape as `migrationSafety.test.ts`, which
exists because a migration once wrote learner state — **this is the second time a
content operation has damaged a learner's own data.**

**The recovery** (`b4129a8`, `56ccde2`), and the reason it was possible at all:
the `session` table survived, carrying both `items_correct` **and**
`skill_planned` per session. So the totals *and* the per-skill attribution were
rebuildable as evidence rather than estimate.

`npm run restore:attempts` — dry-run by default, then `--write`, then
`--attribute`, with `--undo`. Restored rows are stamped
`response.source = 'history_restore'` so the whole repair stays identifiable and
reversible. Cecily's per-skill counts came back across 63 skills; her sight words
read 369 and her subtraction 241 again.

**What could not be recovered:** which individual item she answered. Those rows
are gone and their items were replaced, so restored rows point at a
representative item of the right skill — truthful at the skill level, which is
what the map displays, approximate below it. And ~30 rows from adaptive focus
sessions stay deliberately unattributed, because those sessions record
`focus.math` rather than a skill and there is no honest answer.

**The lesson, stated plainly:** a content operation must never write or erase
learner state, and "the script warns you in a comment" is not a control.

## 3. Four bugs that only a browser could catch

None of these is a logic error. All four passed the entire suite.

| bug | why the tests could not see it |
|---|---|
| **The wrong bird played.** The mourning dove cooing when the question asked about the robin. | An `<audio>` element only reads its `<source>` children once, during resource selection. React reused the node between questions and swapped the `src`, which an already-loaded element ignores. The generator was innocent — 2,040 generated clip exercises across every unit and 60 seeds have zero prompt/clip mismatches, which I checked *before* touching anything. |
| **The spectrogram was cropped**, showing a sliver. | `object-fit: cover` in a fixed-height box. On a photo a crop loses scenery; on a spectrogram the vertical axis is FREQUENCY, so it deletes notes. Uncropped, the pictures turned out to be poor on their own terms too — a linear scale squashes a 500 Hz dove into the bottom pixels — so all 15 were redrawn on a log scale. |
| **A blocked microphone was a dead end.** | Both mic item types gated their fallback on whether speech was *supported*. With speech supported and permission *blocked*, the screen said "use the buttons below" and the button rendered only in the unsupported case. No way to answer, no exit but to leave. |
| **Esme's tier offered a bird she had never met.** | `otherBirds()` leads with `confusableWith`, which crosses crews on purpose. At Level 1 that meant choosing between a chickadee and a White-breasted Nuthatch from a crew she cannot see. |

Three of the four were found by *looking at the running app*; one was found by
asking why a number in the session data looked wrong. Zero were found by tests.
Every one now has a test, and for the first three I confirmed the test fails with
the fix reverted — a regression test that passes either way is worthless.

## 9. The letterbox — a channel pointing the other way

Cecily asked for it in her own words: a way to *"directly message you for this
project, or post somewhere you could read from, to then update things / add
things of her interest."*

Everything in this app had until now been built FOR her by an adult reading her
rows. This is the first thing pointing the other way, and it changed how the rest
of the session worked.

`d1c150b`. A letterbox beside the path in the garden — **not** another header
icon; that header carries a comment saying a row of icons does not scale, and it
is right. She taps it, gets a sheet of paper that begins "Dear garden-builder,"
and writes, or dictates it with the microphone the reading exercises already use.
No categories, no bug/feature dropdown, no "was this helpful". A seven-year-old
writing about her own garden is writing a letter, not filing a ticket.

Replies come back. `npm run letters` reads the box; `--reply <id> "…"` writes
back; a waiting reply **raises the flag on the letterbox** until she opens it.
Stored in `world_state.garden.letters`, so no migration.

A failed send never clears the textarea. Losing a child's letter to a network
blip is the one unforgivable bug in this feature.

**Two letters and two replies are already in it**, and they are the record of how
Crystal Cavern got specified.

## 10. Crystal Cavern — the first feature she commissioned

Her brief, unedited: a gem mine in one of the mountains, a "habitat" inside to
learn how gems form, how they differ, colour, hardness/carats, value, mining and
history, plus maths on money/selling, weight and geometry, opening at Level 3.
Then, through the letterbox: **it is called Crystal Cavern**, her favourite gem
is **ruby**, the creature should be a **salamander** (with cave crickets and bats
too), and gems should *"carry a worth, maybe in money terms, that can be traded
for garden goods (maybe can eventually build garden things?)"*.

Design and sources: `2026-08-06-gem-mine-design.md`.

### Kentucky owns this subject

Sourced, and the local-first rule that shaped the birds applies twice over.
**Kentucky agate is the state mineral AND state gemstone — and for twenty-four
years the state had it backwards**, calling coal a mineral and agate a rock,
until the legislature corrected it in 2024. That is the whole rock-versus-mineral
lesson arriving as a true story in which grown-ups got it wrong. Western Kentucky
was also one of the two most productive fluorite districts in the country, and
the fluorite was **thrown away as waste** for decades before anyone looked at it
properly.

### Ruby forced an honest structure

There are no rubies in Kentucky. Rather than fudge it, the catalog has two
shelves: **the SEAM** (agate, fluorite, geode, coal, quartz, calcite, galena,
freshwater pearl — what is actually under her, and it leads) and **the CASE**
(ruby, sapphire, garnet, emerald, diamond — the famous ones, the way a real
mining museum keeps a display cabinet). Ruby leads the case and turns out to be
the best teacher available: ruby and sapphire are the SAME mineral, the red is
chromium, and the nearest place she could really dig one is a few hours away in
North Carolina.

`mohs` is the load-bearing field, with a scratch test in objects she owns, because
hardness is the one gem property a child can TEST rather than memorise.
`scratchTestFor` returns null for ruby and diamond, and that null is the lesson.

### The economy, and the trap it had to avoid

**This project's documented failure mode is a child farming easy content for
rewards.** A currency that buys things is exactly that shape. So:

- **One dig a day**, enforced server-side — a cap the browser owns is not a cap.
- **No coin is ever paid per correct answer.** Grinding subtract-within-10 all
  afternoon earns nothing here.
- Coins come only from selling, and specimens only from digging.

Every stone offers **keep or sell**, never both — opportunity cost, real maths,
and a better mechanic than either option originally offered to her.

### The creatures are the point, not an exemption

`residents.test.ts` requires any habitat with an interior to attract species, and
a mine sounds like the one habitat with nothing alive in it. The test would have
accepted a widened exemption. The truth was better: the cave salamander has
**large** eyes because it lives where light still reaches, the cricket has eyes
too and leaves at night to carry food back in, the bat sleeps in and feeds out,
and the Kentucky cave shrimp has **no eyes at all** and lives nowhere else on
Earth. Four answers to one question, and the drawings carry the difference.

Digging turns up creatures in order, so **the salamander she asked for is always
the first thing she meets**.

### Proof the seed fix holds

Adding the habitat and creatures needed `npm run db:seed` — the exact command
that destroyed her history in §2. It ran additively and her 2,061 correct answers
were untouched. First real confirmation.

1. **The coins have nothing to buy.** Garden ornaments (a bench, a lantern, a

doc — that is real work, worth doing on its own terms rather than smuggled in
under a gem.
